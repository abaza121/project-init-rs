# SWOT

## Strengths

- The shared mech creates an instantly explainable cooperative hook.
- Damage-driven role swaps bind mechanical failure to communication and comedy.
- A three-minute attempt and quick restart support repeatable prototype playtests.
- Split controller domains make each player dependent without requiring separate avatars.
- One block, one civilian type, and three hazards keep content production bounded.

## Weaknesses

- One player's mistake can disrupt both players, creating blame if feedback is unclear.
- A forced role swap can feel arbitrary unless damage, new ownership, and available actions are unmistakable.
- Shared-keyboard ergonomics may differ materially from two-controller play.
- Comedy and exaggerated physics can undermine television readability if effects obscure hazards or roles.
- The outcome condition and multiplier rules are not yet authorised, so the reward loop cannot be balanced.

## Opportunities

- The action-domain architecture can support varied malfunction patterns without adding characters.
- Short dyad tests can reveal whether mastery and comedy emerge from the same interactions.
- Data-defined hazards can let the team compare candidates after stakeholder selection without restructuring the level.
- Clear objectives, visible progress, and configurable inputs can later widen access if their scope is approved.

## Threats

- Unresolved primary platform choice can invalidate device, performance, packaging, and certification assumptions.
- An unresolved exact target age can change humour, danger depiction, vocabulary, and test recruitment.
- Input remapping and difficulty decisions made too late can require UI and control-system rework.
- Physics variance can create unfair damage cascades or make a three-minute attempt difficult to tune.
- Treating externally sourced guidance as product authority could silently override stakeholder-owned choices.
